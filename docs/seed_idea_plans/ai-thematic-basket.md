# AI as an Investable Theme: From Conviction to a Testable Position

## 1. Statement of the Idea

Artificial intelligence will be transformative, so we should hold long exposure across the
value chain: AI software companies, cloud compute providers, energy suppliers, and
semiconductor manufacturers.

Stated this way, the idea is a conviction about the world, not an investment proposition.
The conviction may well be correct and still lose money, because the return on a position
depends on the difference between what happens and what is already priced, not on what
happens. The purpose of this plan is to convert the conviction into propositions that can
be tested, priced, and either funded or rejected.

## 2. Economic Rationale, Restated Precisely

The claim decomposes into four separable propositions, each of which requires different
evidence:

1. **Aggregate value creation.** AI generates large economic surplus over the coming
   decade. This is plausible and largely irrelevant on its own — the surplus can accrue
   entirely to consumers, to private companies, or to firms not yet listed.
2. **Capture.** Some share of that surplus accrues to the shareholders of specific listed
   companies. Historically, general-purpose technologies have created enormous surplus and
   destroyed most of the capital deployed to build them — railways, airlines, fibre, and
   personal computing all created transformative value and ruinous shareholder returns for
   the majority of participants.
3. **Pricing.** The market has not already discounted that capture in current valuations.
   This is the only proposition that determines the return, and it is the one the seed idea
   does not address at all.
4. **Expression.** A long basket across the named segments is the best available way to own
   the exposure, superior to alternatives such as relative-value positions within the
   theme, positions in the constraints rather than the beneficiaries, or optionality.

The critical observation for the research design is that propositions 1 and 2 are about the
world and are slow to resolve, while proposition 3 is about prices and can be tested now.
The work should be weighted accordingly.

## 3. Hypotheses

- **H1 (theme identifiability).** A rules-based, point-in-time definition of AI exposure
  can be constructed from observable data — revenue disclosure, capital expenditure,
  supply relationships — that is more informative than a name-based or narrative-based
  classification.
- **H2 (factor decomposition).** A naive AI basket's returns are largely explained by
  existing systematic factors — large-cap growth, momentum, quality, and a small number of
  mega-cap idiosyncratic exposures. The residual "AI factor", if it exists, is small.
- **H3 (crowding and valuation).** The theme's valuation dispersion, positioning
  concentration, and flow characteristics are measurable, and current levels can be located
  within their historical distribution and against historical technology cycles.
- **H4 (segment differentiation).** The four named segments have materially different
  economics — pricing power, capital intensity, contract duration, replacement risk — and
  should not be held as a single undifferentiated exposure.
- **H5 (constraint identification).** The binding physical and industrial constraints on AI
  build-out (power generation and grid interconnection, advanced packaging, high-bandwidth
  memory, cooling, skilled construction) are identifiable, and companies controlling scarce
  constrained resources have more durable pricing power than companies competing in
  abundant ones.
- **H6 (implied expectations).** The growth and margin path implied by current prices can
  be reverse-engineered for the major constituents, and compared to a defensible base case
  and to historical realised outcomes for analogous capital cycles.
- **H7 (expression dominance).** At least one alternative expression — long-constraint
  versus short-abundance pairs, exposure through the funding or energy layer, or convexity
  through options — offers a better risk-adjusted profile than the long basket.

## 4. Data and Infrastructure Requirements

- **Point-in-time fundamentals** for the candidate universe: revenue by segment where
  disclosed, capital expenditure, depreciation schedules, order backlog, customer
  concentration, and gross margin history.
- **Point-in-time index membership and sector classifications**, survivorship-free universe
  including delisted and acquired names.
- **Supply chain relationship data**, with history, to build exposure maps that do not rely
  on narrative.
- **Filings text archive** with timestamps, for constructing disclosure-based exposure
  measures and for measuring narrative intensity separately from economic exposure.
- **A vendor or internal risk factor model**, for the decomposition in H2.
- **Positioning and flow data**: fund holdings with history, ETF flows and holdings, short
  interest, and options open interest, for the crowding work in H3.
- **Options surface** on major constituents, for implied-expectation and convexity work.
- **Historical analogue datasets**: constituent-level return, capital expenditure and
  survival data from prior technology and infrastructure capital cycles, for base-rate
  calibration.
- **Energy sector data**: generation capacity, interconnection queues, power purchase
  agreement terms and duration, and regional capacity pricing, for the H5 constraint work.

## 5. Analysis and Construction

**Exposure definition.** Build a rules-based exposure score per company from disclosed
segment revenue, capital expenditure directed at the relevant capacity, supply
relationships, and — separately and explicitly labelled — narrative intensity in filings and
communications. Keeping narrative separate is essential: it allows us to test whether the
market is paying for economics or for storytelling, which is a directly tradeable question.

**Segment taxonomy.** Rather than the four named buckets, classify by economic
characteristics: who has contracted revenue versus spot exposure; who owns a scarce
physical constraint versus a replicable capability; who is a price setter versus a price
taker; and where in the chain capital is being consumed versus returned. Position sizing
follows the economics, not the label.

**Factor decomposition.** Regress candidate basket returns on the risk model's factors plus
a small set of thematic controls. Report the residual. Construct a factor-neutral version of
the basket and compare. If the neutralised basket has no return, the theme is a repackaged
factor exposure and should be bought as such — more cheaply.

**Implied expectations.** For the largest constituents, reverse-engineer the revenue growth,
margin and duration assumptions embedded in current prices under a transparent discounting
framework. Compare against (a) a defensible base case, and (b) the realised outcomes of the
most aggressive comparable historical capital cycles. This is the central valuation test.

**Constraint mapping.** Identify the binding constraints on build-out, quantify the scarcity
(lead times, queue lengths, capacity additions versus demand), and identify listed companies
with durable control of them. Distinguish scarcity that is structural from scarcity that is
merely a lag before supply responds — the latter is a cyclical peak, not a moat.

**Expression comparison.** Construct and price: the naive long basket; the factor-neutral
basket; a long-constraint/short-abundance relative value construction; an energy-layer
expression; and a convexity expression via long-dated options. Compare on expected return
under a scenario set, on drawdown under an adverse scenario, on capital consumed, and on
capacity.

**Scenario set.** Define at least four scenarios with probabilities assigned in advance:
sustained build-out; capital-cycle bust with surplus accruing to users; consolidation with
value concentrating in a few winners; and a regulatory or supply-shock disruption. Every
expression is scored against every scenario.

## 6. Experimental Design

**Stage 0 — Universe and exposure construction.** Build the point-in-time exposure scores.
Validate by checking that the scoring, applied to historical dates, would have identified
the relevant companies using only then-available disclosure. Report how much of today's
obvious membership was actually identifiable ex ante — this is a direct measure of how much
of the theme's past return was capturable.

**Stage 1 — Factor decomposition.** The H2 test. Run before anything else expensive,
because a negative result changes the entire project's premise.

**Stage 2 — Historical base rates.** For prior technology and infrastructure capital cycles,
measure constituent-level outcomes: what fraction of the companies in each layer delivered
returns above the market over ten years, and what fraction of the total value creation was
captured by shareholders of the listed builders versus the users. This is the H2-adjacent
test of "capture" and it is cheap relative to its decision value.

**Stage 3 — Crowding and valuation location.** The H3 test. Locate current valuation
dispersion, positioning concentration and flow intensity within their own history and
against the analogues from Stage 2.

**Stage 4 — Implied expectations.** The H6 test, for the largest constituents individually.
Report the implied growth path against realised historical growth paths for the most
successful comparable companies, so the question becomes "is the price implying an outcome
better than the best historical precedent?"

**Stage 5 — Constraint analysis.** The H5 test, with particular attention to the energy
layer where physical lead times are longest and most measurable.

**Stage 6 — Expression bake-off.** Score all expressions against the scenario set. Test H7.

**Stage 7 — Risk and sizing.** Correlation of each expression with the existing book;
drawdown under the adverse scenario; liquidity of exit; and a sizing recommendation with
pre-specified review triggers and thesis-invalidation conditions.

## 7. Evaluation Metrics and Pre-Registered Criteria

Metrics: ex-ante identifiability of the theme; factor-explained share of basket returns;
residual return of the neutralised basket; historical base rates of shareholder capture in
analogous cycles; current valuation and crowding percentiles versus history; implied growth
rates versus historical best-case realisations; scenario-weighted expected return and
adverse-scenario drawdown per expression; and correlation to the existing book.

Pre-registered criteria:

- **No-go on the naive basket** if factor decomposition shows the majority of its return
  variance and its expected return are explained by existing factor exposures already held
  in the book — in that case the position adds concentration, not exposure.
- **Go** for a specific expression only if it has positive scenario-weighted expected
  return under the pre-assigned probabilities *and* an adverse-scenario drawdown within a
  stated limit.
- **Go** requires that implied expectations for the chosen constituents not exceed the best
  realised outcome from the historical analogue set, unless a specific, documented reason is
  given for why this cycle differs.
- **No-go** if the exposure score's ex-ante identifiability is so poor that the theme could
  only have been recognised in hindsight — that would indicate we are fitting a narrative to
  a realised price path.
- Sizing is capped by the correlation to existing holdings, so the theme cannot be bought
  twice through different labels.

## 8. Deliverables and Timeline

- Weeks 1–3: universe and point-in-time exposure construction.
- Week 4: factor decomposition.
- Weeks 5–6: historical base rates from analogous capital cycles.
- Week 7: crowding and valuation location.
- Weeks 8–9: implied expectations analysis.
- Week 10: constraint mapping, with emphasis on the energy layer.
- Week 11: expression bake-off against the scenario set.
- Week 12: risk, sizing, and investment committee memorandum.

## Critique of the Plan

### The plan improves the idea so thoroughly that it may be answering a different question

The seed idea is a directional conviction and an instruction to buy broadly. The plan
replaces it with a valuation-and-expression study whose most likely conclusion is "the
naive basket is a leveraged bet on factors we already own, and here are three better
constructions". That is almost certainly the more useful piece of work, but it should be
labelled as a reformulation. If the actual decision facing the firm is whether to add
thematic long exposure at all, then twelve weeks of analysis that concludes with a
relative-value construction may not answer the question that was asked, and the person who
asked it will reasonably say so.

### Scenario probabilities are assigned by the same people scoring the scenarios

The expression bake-off is scored on scenario-weighted expected return, with probabilities
"assigned in advance". This is better than not assigning them, but it concentrates the
entire conclusion into four unfalsifiable numbers chosen by the analyst. Whichever
expression wins will be an artefact of those weights. The plan needs a sensitivity analysis
showing, for each expression, the range of probability assignments under which it wins —
and it should identify expressions that are robust across a wide range rather than optimal
under one. As written, the bake-off has the appearance of rigour without the substance.

### The historical base-rate work is the plan's strongest idea and its weakest execution

Measuring shareholder capture in prior technology capital cycles is exactly the right
corrective to the idea's premise, and placing it early is good. But two weeks is not enough
to assemble constituent-level, survivorship-free data on historical capital cycles — that
data is genuinely hard to source, and the earlier the cycle the worse the coverage. The
likely outcome is a handful of hand-picked case studies presented as base rates. If the
data cannot be assembled properly, the plan should say so and treat the analysis as
qualitative reasoning rather than dressing it in the language of measurement.

### Factor decomposition is backward-looking and the theme is not

Stage 1 tests whether the basket's *historical* returns are explained by existing factors,
and a positive result triggers a no-go on the naive basket. But the theme's proponents are
making a forward claim: that the AI exposure will drive future returns whether or not it has
been separable historically. Historical inseparability from growth and momentum factors does
not establish that the exposure is redundant going forward, particularly if the theme is
early. The no-go as written may reject the position for the wrong reason. It should be
narrowed: the finding establishes that *buying the basket today duplicates existing risk*,
which is an argument about portfolio construction and sizing, not an argument that the
thesis is wrong.

### Crowding is measured as a level, and levels have poor timing content

Stage 3 locates current valuation and positioning within their historical distributions.
Extreme percentiles are a genuinely useful risk input, and a poor timing input — themes have
persisted at extreme valuation percentiles for years. The plan does not say how the crowding
result feeds any decision. Without an explicit mapping from crowding percentile to sizing or
to expression choice, the stage produces a number that will be quoted in the memo and will
not change anything.

### The energy constraint work is under-resourced relative to its importance

H5 identifies power as a binding constraint, and the plan allocates one week to constraint
mapping across power, packaging, memory, cooling and construction. The power layer alone
involves regional interconnection queues, capacity market mechanics, long-dated contract
structures, and regulatory processes that differ by jurisdiction — and it is the segment
where the idea's original framing is most likely to be either right or wrong for
non-obvious reasons. Either the constraint work is narrowed to power and given three weeks,
or the plan should acknowledge it will be a survey rather than an analysis.

### Narrative intensity is measured but never traded

Separating disclosed economic exposure from narrative intensity is a genuinely good idea,
and the plan explicitly flags that it makes a tradeable question available: is the market
paying for economics or for storytelling. Having identified that, the plan then never
constructs the corresponding test or expression. A long-economics/short-narrative
construction is an obvious candidate for the bake-off and is missing from the list of
expressions to be priced.

### The identifiability criterion may be too strict to survive

The no-go on poor ex-ante identifiability is well motivated — it guards against fitting a
narrative to a realised path. But applied honestly, almost no thematic exposure is cleanly
identifiable ex ante from disclosure, because segment reporting lags the business and
companies describe themselves opportunistically. The criterion as written will likely fire,
which either kills a project that should not be killed or, more probably, gets quietly
softened when it does fire. A threshold that everyone expects to be renegotiated is worse
than no threshold. It should be replaced with a requirement to report identifiability and
to discount expected returns accordingly.

### There is no treatment of the position's behaviour in the firm's existing book

The plan measures correlation to existing holdings and caps sizing by it, which is right,
but only at Stage 7. If the book already carries substantial large-cap growth and
semiconductor exposure — which is likely for almost any equity book of scale — then the
honest conclusion may be that the firm is already long this theme by a considerable margin
and the correct action is to reduce, not add. That possibility is never contemplated
anywhere in the plan, and it is the single most likely correct answer.

### Timeline and staffing are not matched to the breadth

The plan spans point-in-time fundamental data engineering, factor modelling, financial
history research, options analysis, and industrial and energy sector expertise. That is
several people's work across several disciplines, presented as a twelve-week sequential
schedule. Without a named team it will be executed shallowly, and the shallow parts will be
the industrial and energy analysis — precisely the parts where a non-consensus insight is
most likely to be found.

### What the plan gets right

Separating the conviction into value creation, capture, pricing and expression, and
observing that only pricing determines the return, is the correct diagnosis of what is wrong
with the idea as stated. Insisting on historical base rates for shareholder capture in prior
capital cycles brings evidence to bear on the assumption most likely to be wrong. Reverse-
engineering implied expectations and testing them against the best realised historical
outcome converts a vague valuation debate into a specific falsifiable question. Reclassifying
the segments by economic characteristics rather than by label prevents the position from
being sized by narrative. And capping sizing by correlation to the existing book directly
addresses the way thematic exposure is most often accidentally doubled.

## My Verdict

### Plan

This idea tried to capture something that might well be directionally right (AI will be transformative) but with a flawed investment proposition (go long everything across the value chain) and Claude spotted this. As a result Claude tried to reformulate the problem into a much more sophisticated investment thesis which may (not) have been desired by the user.

### Critique

The critique highlights that the research plan has moved materially from the posed idea.

Having acknowledged this the concerns highlighted are reasonable.
